Field note

Asian weeknights

Thai, Chinese and South Asian recipes retested on a domestic range and a US supermarket shelf, with every shortcut named and every substitution measured.

Asia is not a cuisine, and a page called Asian Weeknights is a compromise we will state plainly. What these recipes share is not a tradition. It is a set of shelf-stable ingredients that build depth in under forty minutes, plus a reliance on high heat and short cook times that fits a Tuesday.

We use store-bought curry paste and we say so. A paste pounded in a granite mortar is better and costs forty minutes of work, and almost nobody spends that on a work night, cooks in Bangkok included. The step we will not drop is frying the paste before any liquid goes in. In side by side batches, the fried-paste version was picked by every tester in the room. It is also the step most published recipes skip.

Where a dish belongs to a specific tradition we name it. Where we adapted it, we say what changed and what it cost.

Nine ingredients, and what each one does

Nine items cover most of what these recipes call for. All nine keep for months, and all nine are stocked at a well-supplied Kroger, an H Mart or a 99 Ranch, with most of them at Trader Joe's. Keep times below are what we observed in a 38°F refrigerator or a cupboard away from the range, not manufacturer claims.

Three techniques, three measurements

Fry the paste for two minutes, do not dissolve it

Two minutes of curry paste pressed into hot oil, before any liquid, moves the flavor from sharp and raw to round and toasted. You are looking for the oil to separate and turn the color of the paste. It is the same mechanism as cooking out tomato paste in an Italian sauce, and it is worth the two minutes every time.

Cut everything before the burner goes on

A stir-fry runs about four minutes at high heat. There is no gap in the middle to slice a pepper. Cut everything, line the bowls up in the order they go in, then light the burner. On a home range this matters more than on a restaurant wok burner, because a domestic flame recovers heat slowly and every pause costs you the sear.

Season off the heat, at the end

Fish sauce and soy sauce concentrate as liquid reduces, so a dish seasoned to taste at minute two will be over-salted at minute twenty. Taste at the end, off the heat, and correct in two directions: fish sauce for salt, lime for brightness. Add a teaspoon at a time.

What goes wrong

  • Curry that tastes thin and raw. The paste went in with the coconut milk instead of before it. Fry it in oil first.
  • Coconut sauce that splits into oil and grit. The pan boiled hard. Hold it at a bare simmer once the milk is in.
  • Steamed, gray stir-fry. The pan was crowded or the protein was wet. Cook in two batches and pat everything dry.
  • Noodles that turn to paste. Dried rice noodles were boiled rather than soaked. Follow the soak time on the packet, then finish them in the sauce.
  • Bitter, acrid finish. Toasted sesame oil went into a hot pan. It is a finishing oil, added off the heat.

Allergen note

Fish sauce contains anchovy, a fish allergen. Many red curry pastes contain shrimp paste, a shellfish allergen, and oyster sauce contains shellfish. Soy sauce contains soy and, unless labeled gluten-free, wheat. Read the label every time if you are cooking for someone with an allergy, and see our allergen disclaimer.

Where to shop

An Asian supermarket is cheaper and better stocked than the international aisle of a general grocery store, usually by a wide margin. If there is an H Mart, a 99 Ranch, a Patel Brothers or a local Vietnamese or Chinese grocer near you, that is the trip worth making.

If not, most large Kroger and Safeway stores carry soy sauce, fish sauce, coconut milk, curry paste and rice noodles. Trader Joe's carries a workable coconut milk and rice noodles, and a narrow range of pastes.

Portland, OR

From our own kitchen: Fubonn on SE 82nd and the Uwajimaya in Beaverton cover everything on the table above under one roof, and both carry fresh rice noodles, which beat dried for texture and need no soaking. Hong Phat on SE Powell is the cheaper stop for fish sauce and dried noodles by the case. For fresh curry leaves and whole spices, the Indian grocers along SE Division restock midweek.
