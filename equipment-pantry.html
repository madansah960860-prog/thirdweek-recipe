Equipment & pantry staples

Twelve tools, the three where price actually buys performance, and the pantry behind every recipe on this site. Prices are what we paid, in USD.

Almost every recipe on this site is cooked with about a dozen tools. We have run a lot of equipment through this kitchen and the result is consistent: at most price points the expensive version performs the same as the mid-priced one. There are three exceptions and they are marked below.

Prices are approximate US retail in USD and they move, so read them as a range and not a quote.

Twelve tools, in order of how often we reach for them

8-inch chef's knife$40 to $60Does 90 percent of the cutting in any kitchen. Buy one that feels balanced in your hand rather than one with a famous name. Keep it sharp.
Large wooden or plastic cutting board$25 to $50Bigger than you think you need. A small board means food falls off it and you work slowly.

5 or 6 quart Dutch oven$60 to $350Braises, stews, soup, bread. This is one of the three places where the expensive version genuinely lasts longer.
Instant-read thermometer$15 to $100The single most useful $15 in cooking. It ends guessing about meat, bread and oil. The expensive ones read faster; the cheap ones read correctly.

Tongs, 12 inch$10Your hand, extended, heatproof. Buy the ones that lock closed.

Where spending more genuinely helps

  • Dutch oven. Enamelled cast iron from a good maker holds heat evenly and the enamel survives decades. Cheap enamel chips within a year or two. If you buy one expensive thing, buy this.
  • Sheet pans. Not because the good ones cost much, but because thin ones buckle in a 425°F oven and throw hot oil around. Heavy-gauge aluminum is about $18.
  • Knife sharpening. Not a tool, a habit. A $40 knife that is sharp outperforms a $200 knife that is dull, every time. A pull-through sharpener is $20 and adequate; a whetstone is better and takes practice.

What you can skip

  • Garlic press. A microplane does it better and washes more easily.
  • Knife blocks with fourteen knives. You will use three of them. The rest are storage.
  • Single-purpose gadgets: avocado slicers, strawberry hullers, egg separators, banana slicers. A knife and a spoon do all of it.
  • Non-stick pans over $50. The coating is a consumable. It degrades in two to five years no matter what you paid. Buy a cheap one and replace it.
  • A stand mixer, at first. It is excellent and it is $400. A hand mixer costs $30 and does everything except knead heavy dough.

How we test equipment

Nothing reaches this list from a press release or a spec sheet. A tool goes into normal service in the Portland kitchen for at least three months of ordinary cooking before we write about it, and where a direct comparison is possible we run one: sheet pans loaded with the same tray of vegetables at 425°F to see which buckle, thermometers checked against an ice bath at 32°F and boiling water, scales checked against a 500 gram calibration weight. Two of the twelve items above replaced earlier picks that failed in use. We say so on the page when that happens rather than quietly swapping the link.

What actually breaks

  • Thin sheet pans. They buckle with a bang in a hot oven and throw fat. Heavy-gauge aluminum solves it for about $18.
  • Cheap enamel on a Dutch oven. Chips at the rim inside a year or two, and once it chips the exposed iron rusts.
  • Non-stick coating. A consumable, not a feature. It degrades in two to five years regardless of price, which is the argument for buying cheap and replacing.
  • Wooden-handled knives left in water. The handle swells, then splits at the tang. Wash and dry knives by hand, immediately.
  • Scales with no tare, or 5 gram resolution. Useless for baking. Check the spec before buying, not after.

The pantry

This is the stock that makes cooking possible without a shopping trip. With all of it in the cupboard, dinner on an unplanned night is an assembly problem rather than a supply problem.

Oils, acids and salts

  • Olive oil, two bottles. A large everyday bottle for cooking, roughly $12 for a liter, and a smaller bottle of something good for finishing, $18 to $30. Do not cook with the expensive one; heat destroys what you paid for.
  • Neutral oil for high heat: canola, grapeseed or vegetable oil.
  • Vinegars: red wine, apple cider and rice vinegar cover almost everything. Balsamic if you like it.
  • Kosher salt in a box, kept in an open container next to the stove so you can pinch it. This one change improves seasoning more than any technique.
  • Flaky sea salt for finishing. A box lasts a year.
  • Black peppercorns and a grinder. Pre-ground pepper loses its aroma within weeks.

Cans and jars

  • Whole peeled tomatoes and crushed tomatoes
  • Tomato paste, ideally in a tube so you can use a tablespoon without opening a can
  • Canned beans: white, black and chickpeas
  • Canned tuna in olive oil
  • Anchovies in oil
  • Coconut milk, full fat
  • Chipotles in adobo
  • Olives and capers

Dry goods

  • Dried pasta in three shapes: long, short and ribbon
  • Rice: long-grain and something short-grain
  • A grain: farro, quinoa or pearl barley
  • Dried lentils, which cook in 25 minutes with no soaking
  • All-purpose flour, kept in a sealed container
  • Granulated and brown sugar
  • Rolled oats
  • Panko breadcrumbs, which stay crisp far better than fine breadcrumbs

Sauces and pastes

  • Soy sauce, naturally brewed
  • Fish sauce
  • Red or green curry paste
  • Dijon mustard
  • Toasted sesame oil, refrigerated after opening
  • Chili crisp or chili oil

Spices worth having, and how long they last

Ground spices lose their aroma after about a year. Whole spices last two to three years. If you cannot smell it clearly when you open the jar, it is not doing anything in your food.

  • Cumin, ground and whole seeds
  • Smoked paprika and sweet paprika
  • Dried oregano
  • Red pepper flakes
  • Ground coriander
  • Cinnamon, ground and sticks
  • Bay leaves
  • Ground turmeric

Buy spices in small quantities from a shop with turnover rather than large jars from a supermarket. A Mexican, Indian or Middle Eastern grocery will sell you a bag of cumin for a third of the supermarket price and it will be fresher.

Refrigerator and freezer

  • Parmesan or Pecorino in the piece, wrapped in parchment then foil. Keeps two months.
  • Unsalted butter. Salted is fine for toast; unsalted lets you control seasoning.
  • Eggs
  • Lemons and limes
  • Frozen peas, corn and spinach
  • Frozen shrimp, which is what most fresh shrimp at a counter was anyway
  • Bread, sliced and frozen, toasted from frozen
  • Parmesan rinds in a bag, dropped into any soup or braise

Where we buy

Restaurant supply stores are open to the public in most US cities and sell sheet pans, tongs and stainless bowls at a fraction of cookware-shop prices, in better quality. Half the pans in this kitchen came from one.

Costco is good value for olive oil, nuts, cheese and flour. Trader Joe's suits pantry staples in small households. A local Mexican, Indian or Asian grocery beats all of them on spices, dried chilies, rice and sauces.

Portland, OR

Locally that means the restaurant supply houses in inner Southeast for pans and tongs, the tiendas on SE 82nd for chilies and spices, and Fubonn for soy sauce, fish sauce and rice by the bag. Oregon has no sales tax, which is worth remembering on a single large purchase such as a Dutch oven.
