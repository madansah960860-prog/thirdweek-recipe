Seasonal produce guide

What is worth buying month by month on the US growing calendar, what it costs at peak, and how to store it. Northern Hemisphere seasons, written from a Pacific Northwest kitchen.

This guide follows the US growing year. The country covers enough latitude that a month meaning asparagus in Georgia means bare ground in Montana, so treat this as a national calendar and not a local one. We write it from Portland, Oregon, where most crops run two to four weeks behind the southeast and where the wet spring pushes berries and tomatoes later than the national average. Your own market is a better source than any website, this one included. Ask the vendor what is good this week and they will tell you.

Two practical reasons to cook seasonally, neither of them moral. The first is that produce at its peak needs less work: a July tomato needs salt and olive oil, a February tomato needs a recipe. The second is price. A vegetable at the height of its local season is usually the cheapest item in the store, often by half.

There is nothing wrong with imported produce out of season, or with frozen vegetables, which are picked and frozen within hours and beat most fresh options between December and March. This is a guide to what tastes best and costs least, not a set of rules.

Jump to a month

January

Citrus season is the reason to cook in January. Cara cara and blood oranges arrive from California and Texas, and they are cheap, sweet and at their best right now.

At its best

  • Citrus: navel, cara cara and blood oranges, grapefruit, Meyer lemons
  • Storage crops: potatoes, onions, winter squash, sweet potatoes
  • Hardy greens: kale, collards, cabbage, Brussels sprouts
  • Roots: carrots, parsnips, turnips, beets, celery root
  • Leeks and shallots

What we cook

Blood orange with fennel and olives. Braised cabbage. Anything roasted at 425°F for a long time.

Worth knowing. Winter squash bought in October is still good now if it was held somewhere cool and dry. Check the stem: soft stem, it is going. In the Pacific Northwest, January is also the low point for local greens, and the overwintered kale that does show up at Portland markets has usually been through several frosts, which makes it sweeter than the summer crop.

February

The thinnest month for US produce almost everywhere. This is when the freezer and the pantry earn their keep, and when citrus is still excellent.

At its best

  • Citrus, still at its peak
  • Cabbage, kale, collards
  • Stored roots and potatoes
  • Mushrooms, which are not seasonal but are always good
  • Frozen peas, corn and green beans, frozen at harvest

What we cook

Braises, soups, slow-cooker dishes, citrus salads.

Worth knowing. Frozen vegetables are picked and frozen within hours and are usually better in February than fresh ones flown in from the southern hemisphere. There is no reason to feel apologetic about them.

March

The first real sign of spring in most of the country. Asparagus starts moving up from the south, and greens come back.

At its best

  • Asparagus, from California and the southeast
  • Spring onions and scallions
  • Spinach, arugula, chard
  • Radishes
  • Artichokes, at their spring peak in California
  • Rhubarb, in the north

What we cook

Asparagus roasted at high heat. Anything with lemon. The first salads that feel like salads.

Worth knowing. Asparagus loses sugar fast after cutting. Buy it with tight tips and firm stalks, stand it in an inch of water in the refrigerator, and cook it within two days. Local asparagus reaches Portland markets later than this, usually in April, so March spears here have travelled from California or Washington's Columbia Basin.

April

Spring properly starts. Asparagus is everywhere and cheap, peas arrive, and the first strawberries appear in Florida and California.

At its best

  • Asparagus, at its cheapest
  • English peas and snap peas
  • Spring garlic and green garlic
  • Lettuces and tender greens
  • Strawberries, in the south
  • Morels, if you are lucky and know where to look

What we cook

Pasta with peas and mint. Asparagus with eggs. Very simple salads.

Worth knowing. Green garlic looks like a scallion and tastes like mild garlic. It is only around for about six weeks and it is worth building a meal on.

May

Strawberries move north and the market starts to look generous again. Everything is tender and nothing needs much doing to it.

At its best

  • Strawberries, through most of the country
  • Asparagus, finishing
  • Peas, favas and broad beans
  • New potatoes
  • Spring onions, radishes, lettuces
  • Rhubarb

What we cook

Strawberries with nothing on them. New potatoes with butter and salt. Panzanella once tomatoes catch up.

Worth knowing. Strawberries do not ripen after picking. A pale shoulder stays pale. Buy them fragrant, buy them local if you can, and eat them within two days. Oregon strawberry varieties are soft, fully red through the center and bruise in transit, which is why they rarely ship. At a Portland market in late May they are worth the trip and they will not survive a third day in the refrigerator.

June

The month everything starts at once. Berries, summer squash, the first tomatoes in the south, cucumbers, sweet corn beginning.

At its best

  • Blueberries, raspberries, cherries
  • Zucchini and summer squash
  • Cucumbers
  • Green beans
  • Sweet corn, starting in the south
  • Tomatoes, in warm regions
  • Basil and soft herbs

What we cook

Grilled zucchini. The first tomato sandwich of the year. Cherry anything.

Worth knowing. Zucchini is at its best when it is small. A zucchini the size of a baseball bat has been left on the plant too long and will be watery and full of seed.

July

Peak. If you only shop at a farmers market once a year, make it a Saturday in July.

At its best

  • Tomatoes, at their best
  • Sweet corn
  • Stone fruit: peaches, nectarines, plums, apricots
  • Blackberries and blueberries
  • Peppers, sweet and hot
  • Eggplant
  • Melons

What we cook

Panzanella. Corn cut off the cob and barely cooked. Peaches with nothing at all.

Worth knowing. Never refrigerate tomatoes. Below about 55°F (13°C) they stop producing the aroma compounds that make them taste like tomatoes, and it does not come back when they warm up.

August

Peak continues, with more of everything and lower prices. This is the month to preserve, freeze and roast whole trays of things.

At its best

  • Tomatoes, in quantity and cheap
  • Peppers, including poblano and shishito
  • Eggplant
  • Sweet corn, at its cheapest
  • Peaches and plums
  • Green beans
  • Okra, in the south

What we cook

Roasted pepper anything. Tomato sauce for the freezer. Grilled eggplant.

Worth knowing. August is when a case of tomatoes at a farmers market costs less per pound than a can. Roast them, blend them, freeze them flat in bags, and cook from them until February. Portland-area vendors sell seconds by the twenty-pound flat through August, and a bruised tomato headed for a roasting pan is worth exactly as much as a perfect one.

September

The overlap month. Summer produce is still around and the first winter squash and apples arrive.

At its best

  • Late tomatoes
  • Peppers, still cheap
  • Apples, early varieties
  • Winter squash: delicata, acorn, the first butternut
  • Sweet corn, finishing
  • Grapes and figs
  • Broccoli and cauliflower returning

What we cook

Roasted squash soup. Apple anything. The last tomato salad of the year.

Worth knowing. Delicata squash has thin, edible skin, which means no peeling. Halve it, scoop the seeds, slice into half moons and roast at 425°F for 25 minutes. It is the easiest winter squash there is.

October

Apples, squash and brassicas take over. The light changes and so does the cooking.

At its best

  • Apples and pears, at their peak
  • Winter squash of every kind
  • Brussels sprouts, on the stalk
  • Cauliflower and broccoli
  • Sweet potatoes
  • Cranberries, from Wisconsin and Massachusetts
  • Wild mushrooms

What we cook

Roasted squash. Brussels sprouts at high heat. Braises starting again.

Worth knowing. Buy hard squash now and store it somewhere cool and dark. Butternut and acorn keep two to three months, which is why they were a staple crop long before refrigeration. October is also chanterelle season in the Oregon Coast Range and the Cascades, and Portland markets price them well below what the same mushroom costs dried in a supermarket.

November

Root vegetables, brassicas and the first hard frosts, which actually improve kale and parsnips by converting starch to sugar.

At its best

  • Brussels sprouts, kale, collards, cabbage
  • Parsnips, turnips, celery root, rutabaga
  • Sweet potatoes and potatoes
  • Winter squash
  • Cranberries
  • Pears, late varieties
  • Leeks

What we cook

Slow-cooker anything. Roasted roots. Braised cabbage.

Worth knowing. Kale and parsnips harvested after a frost are noticeably sweeter. If a market vendor tells you their kale is post-frost, that is a real claim and worth paying for.

December

Citrus arrives again and closes the loop. Storage crops carry the rest of the month.

At its best

  • Citrus, beginning: satsumas, clementines, grapefruit
  • Pomegranates
  • Winter squash and sweet potatoes
  • Cabbage, kale, Brussels sprouts
  • Stored roots and onions
  • Chestnuts

What we cook

Citrus and fennel salads. Long braises. Anything that fills a house with smell.

Worth knowing. Satsuma season is short, roughly six weeks. They are the easiest citrus to peel and the best for eating out of hand, and they do not keep well, so buy small amounts often.

Buying and storing produce well

Where to shop

A farmers market is usually the best produce and rarely the cheapest, the exception being high summer, when a case of tomatoes or peppers is a genuine bargain. Kroger and Safeway carry reliable everyday produce. Trader Joe's is strong on packaged and pre-cut items and weak on loose seasonal produce. Costco is good value when you will use five pounds of something and expensive waste when you will not.

Portland, OR

Where we shop: the Saturday market at Portland State runs year round and is the widest selection in the city. The Hollywood and Montavilla markets are smaller, closer to a neighborhood scale and usually cheaper for volume. For preserving quantities, ask a vendor about seconds and about a case price before the stall is picked over, which in practice means arriving in the first hour.

What not to refrigerate

  • Tomatoes. Cold destroys the aroma permanently. Counter, out of direct sun, stem side down.
  • Potatoes, onions, garlic, winter squash. Cool, dark and dry. Refrigeration turns potato starch to sugar and they brown strangely when cooked.
  • Basil. It blackens below about 50°F (10°C). Stand it in a glass of water on the counter.
  • Unripe stone fruit and avocados. Ripen on the counter first, then refrigerate to hold them.
  • Bread. Refrigeration makes it stale several times faster than the counter. Freeze it instead.

What to refrigerate immediately

  • Leafy greens and herbs other than basil. Wash, dry thoroughly, and store with a paper towel in a container.
  • Berries. Do not wash until you are eating them; surface moisture accelerates mold.
  • Asparagus and green beans. Asparagus stands in an inch of water like flowers.
  • Corn in the husk. Sugar converts to starch quickly. Cook it the day you buy it if you can.
  • Mushrooms. In a paper bag, never plastic, which traps moisture and makes them slimy.

Reading this calendar for your own region

A national month is an average, and the gap between regions is wider than most guides admit. Three adjustments make this page usable wherever you are.

  • Shift for latitude. Add two to four weeks to spring crops as you move north, and subtract about the same in the southeast. Asparagus, peas and strawberries move the most. Storage crops barely move at all.
  • Shift for rain, not just temperature. A cool wet spring delays tomatoes and peppers far more than a cool dry one. In the Willamette Valley that regularly means field tomatoes in August rather than July.
  • Trust the price, not the sign. When something is genuinely in local season the price drops hard, usually by a third or more. A vegetable at full price in its supposed peak month has been shipped.

Produce safety

Wash all fresh produce under running water before eating or cutting it, including items with rinds you do not eat, such as melons, because a knife carries surface bacteria into the flesh. FDA guidance is to use running water rather than soap or produce washes. Scrub firm produce with a clean brush, dry with a clean towel, and cut away bruised areas. Bagged greens labeled pre-washed do not need rewashing, though rewashing does no harm. Keep produce separate from raw meat, poultry and seafood on the counter, in the cart and in the refrigerator.

Cook with what is in season

  • Roasted vegetable traybake
  • Roasted squash and feta soup
  • Panzanella, for tomato season
  • Sheet-pan salmon with zucchini
