The test kitchen, and the standard it works to

Three people, one kitchen on NE Alberta Street, and one rule that decides what ships: nothing is published until it has been cooked at least three times, once by somebody who did not write it.

What Saltgrain is

Saltgrain is a test kitchen. Three people work out of a converted ground-floor unit on NE Alberta Street in Portland, Oregon, and the output is a recipe archive plus the notes that produced it. The name is the unit of the work: the smallest change that moves a dish, measured and written down. It started in 2021 as a shared log of what actually worked, and it is now read by people who are not us.

We publish weeknight cooking for a US kitchen, a US supermarket and a US oven. Grams sit beside cups. Pan diameters are stated. Oven racks are numbered from the bottom. Internal temperatures are given as ranges with a target, because a probe reading is a fact and "until golden" is not.

We are not chefs and we do not claim to be. Two of us cooked professionally and neither does now. What we are is repeatable. We cook a thing until it stops failing, we record the round it failed on, and when we do not know something we write that down instead of guessing in print.

THE STANDARD

Nothing publishes until it has been cooked three times

Once by the developer, once again after the first round is corrected, and at least once by somebody who did not write it and has only the written method to work from. If any round fails, the count resets.

Round one: does the idea hold

The developer cooks it as drafted and logs what happened: pan size, rack position, oven temperature, timings at each stage, and the point where it went off. Round one is expected to fail somewhere. A draft that works first time gets tested harder, not trusted faster.

Round two: fix and re-measure

One variable moves at a time. If a crust stayed pale we change the rack position or the oven temperature, not both, and we cook it again. Every number that changes between rounds is recorded, so we can say later which change did the work.

Round three: a cook who did not write it

The third cook gets the method as written and nothing else. No verbal shortcuts, no standing over the pan. This is where most errors surface, because it is the only round that catches an instruction which made sense inside the writer's head. Nine times out of ten the correction is a missing pan diameter or an unstated visual cue.

Supermarket ingredients only

We test with what is on the shelf at a Fred Meyer, a Safeway or a Trader Joe's. Where a specialty ingredient measurably changes the outcome we say so and give a validated substitution, meaning we cooked the substitute version too. We do not build a weeknight recipe around something you have to order online.

Failure modes named out loud

If a step is easy to get wrong, the recipe says how it goes wrong, what it looks like when it does, and whether it is recoverable. Most recipe writing describes success only. The description of the failure is the part people actually need at 7pm.

Retested when readers report a problem

If several people report the same fault, it goes back on the bench. Every recipe carries a last-tested date and that date moves whenever the method does. A recipe that has sat for two years without revision is not proven stable, it is simply untouched, and we would rather you knew which.

What the three-test rule does not cover

Three rounds in one kitchen is a floor, not a guarantee. Our ovens run true to within about 10°F because we check them against a hanging thermometer every quarter, and yours may not. Our tap water is soft, which matters for bread and for boiled greens. Our altitude is roughly 50 feet, so sea-level timings apply here and high-altitude cooks should expect longer bakes and faster boils. Where a recipe is known to be sensitive to any of those, we say so in the method rather than letting you find out.

MASTHEAD

Who does what

Three people, three jobs, and a deliberate rule that the person who develops a recipe is never the person who signs off on it.

June Hasegawa

Editor. June runs the publishing queue and holds the sign-off. Nothing goes live until she has seen the test log for all three rounds and can point to the round that failed and the change that fixed it. She spent nine years as a copy editor on reference titles before moving into food, which is where the house style comes from: weights beside volumes, pan sizes in inches, no instruction that depends on knowing what the writer meant. She is also the person who kills a recipe when the third test does not hold.

Priya Raghunathan

Lead recipe developer. Priya writes the first draft and runs rounds one and two. She trained as a pastry cook, which is why every baking recipe on this site carries gram weights and a dough temperature, and why the savory recipes increasingly do too. Her testing notes are the source of most of the failure-mode paragraphs you read in a method. Ask her about volume measurement for flour and set aside twenty minutes.

Wes Brandt

Photography and production. Wes shoots every dish on the site and, more usefully, shoots the failures: the pale crust, the broken sauce, the crowded pan. He is also our third cook, which means he is the one working from the written method with nothing else to go on. Six years behind a produce counter on SE Division gave him the opinions about tomatoes and stone fruit that turn up in our sourcing guides. Reader mail lands with him first.

How this site is funded

Every recipe here is free to read and stays that way. Free to read is not free to produce. A three-round test means the groceries get bought three times, photography runs about a day per recipe, and hosting and email bill every month whether or not anyone visits. Below is where the money comes from, itemised, with nothing buried in a footer.

Display advertising

Ads run between sections and in the sidebar on recipe and article pages. Each one is labeled as an advertisement. None sit above the recipe itself, none expand over the method, and there are no ads at all on our legal pages or the error page. This is the largest single line in our income.

Affiliate commissions

When we link to a pan, an appliance or a pantry item, that link may be an affiliate link, which means we may earn a commission if you buy through it. It costs you nothing extra. The disclosure sits next to the link itself, every time, not only in the footer. We do not accept payment for a recommendation. A product does not reach our equipment page unless it is in this kitchen and has been used through at least one full test cycle. Full details are in the advertising and affiliate disclosure.

Paid digital products

We sell downloadable cookbooks at $14 and seasonal meal-plan bundles at $9, priced in USD. They collect and extend material that is largely free here, laid out for printing, with prep schedules and editable shopping lists. Buying one is optional. Nothing is held back from the free site to make the paid one look better.

Optional membership

A membership tier at $5 per month removes ads, adds printable recipe cards for every recipe, and lets you save collections to your account. The free site stays complete. Membership buys a quieter page, not a better recipe.

Sponsored and brand-partnered recipes

Occasionally a company pays us to develop a recipe around their product. That page carries a sponsorship label at the very top, above the headline, before you read a word of the method. The recipe still clears three rounds of testing. Sponsors do not review our copy, cannot change a temperature or a timing, and cannot require a positive verdict. If we cannot produce something that passes on its own merits, we do not publish and we return the fee. We have done that twice.

Newsletter sponsorship

The Thursday newsletter carries at most one clearly marked sponsor slot per issue. Everything else in the email is editorial and was written by us. Media kit, rates and audience figures are on the Work With Us page.

What we will not do

  • Publish a paid review as though it were an independent one.
  • Let a sponsor change a method, a temperature, a timing or a verdict.
  • Publish a recipe that has not cleared three rounds, sponsored or not.
  • Run pop-ups that block a recipe, auto-playing video with sound, or anything that hijacks your back button.
  • Sell your email address, or share it with a sponsor.
  • Make health, medical or weight claims, or describe food as clean, guilt-free or sinful.

What we do not do

We are cooks, not dietitians, doctors or nutritionists. We do not give nutrition advice, we do not frame recipes around calorie restriction or body weight, and we make no claims about what a dish does to your health. Where a nutrition estimate appears it sits at the end of the recipe, labeled as an estimate, and the nutrition and allergen disclaimer explains why you should treat it as one.

We do not claim authority we have not earned. When we cook from a tradition that is not ours we name the tradition, name what we changed and say why the change was made. Usually the why is a test result: a shorter soak, a different pan, a lower oven.

We do not publish a number we have not measured. If a temperature, a yield or a timing appears on this site, somebody in this kitchen recorded it off a probe, a scale or a timer.

If we get something wrong, tell us. Corrections go on the recipe itself with a revised date, not into a quiet edit.
