Week 38

Meal plans & shopping lists

Three weeks of dinners, five nights each, with one shopping list per week sorted by aisle so you cross the store once instead of three times.

A plan only holds if it accounts for the fact that you will be tired on Wednesday. These three weeks are built to that constraint: the longest cook sits on Sunday, the shortest night sits midweek, and at least one dinner runs on something already in the refrigerator.

Each plan covers five dinners rather than seven. Most households eat out, order in or graze on leftovers at least twice a week, and a seven-night plan fails on day four. We would rather publish the number that survives contact with a real week.

The lists below are compiled from the recipes in each plan. Quantities are combined where an ingredient appears twice, and items are grouped by the section of the store that stocks them. Tick items as you shop. The list holds your ticks if you close the page.

All of it is free. The printable version with recipe cards is part of our seasonal meal-plan bundles at $9.

Lemon, lime and chili carry this week. Nothing runs past an hour, and Thursday is set at twenty minutes on purpose, because that is the night testers reported abandoning the plan.

How to build your own week

If none of these fits your household, the structure underneath them is five rules and copies cleanly.

  1. Pick one long cook and put it on the day you have most time. A braise, a slow cooker or a big traybake. This is the meal that generates leftovers.
  2. Pick one meal that uses those leftovers in a different form, two or three days later. Different form matters; the same dish twice in a week feels like a punishment, the same sauce in a different dish does not.
  3. Put your fastest recipe on your worst day. For most people that is Wednesday or Thursday. Twenty minutes, maximum.
  4. Include one meal built entirely from the pantry so that a missed shopping trip does not derail the week.
  5. Leave two nights empty. Plans that account for every night are the ones that collapse.

Making the shopping list work

Four habits, each of which cut minutes off our own test shops.

  • Group by aisle, not by recipe. Walking back to produce three times is how a twenty-minute shop becomes forty.
  • Combine duplicates before you leave. Two recipes each calling for half an onion means one onion, not two.
  • Check the pantry section last, at home. Most of what a recipe lists as pantry you already own, and buying a fourth jar of cumin is how cupboards fill up.
  • Buy herbs for the week, not the recipe. One bunch of parsley covers three dishes if you plan for it, and half a bunch always rots.

How we tested these plans

Each week was shopped and cooked start to finish by two households before it went up, one cooking for two and one for four. We logged the actual clock time for every dinner, not the recipe estimate, and moved any night that ran more than ten minutes over. Week one originally put the scampi on Wednesday. Both households cooked something else instead, so it moved to Friday. The cost ranges are receipt totals from Portland-area supermarkets, rounded, and they will run lower in the Midwest and higher in the Northeast.

Where a week goes wrong

  • The long cook lands on a short day. Check the Sunday recipe start time before you shop, not at 5pm on the day.
  • Herbs bought per recipe. Two half-bunches rot. Buy one bunch and plan the three dishes that use it.
  • Leftovers eaten on day five. USDA guidance caps cooked leftovers at three to four days. Freeze on day two if Thursday is uncertain.
  • Noodles cooked in the soup. They keep swelling in the container. Cook them separately whenever a dish is meant to produce leftovers.
  • No pantry night. One missed shopping trip takes down the whole week. Keep one dinner that needs nothing fresh.

Storing a week of food safely

Get perishables into the refrigerator within two hours of shopping, or one hour above 90°F (32°C). Keep raw meat, poultry and seafood on the lowest shelf in a container so nothing drips onto ready-to-eat food. Cooked leftovers keep three to four days at 40°F (4°C) or below under USDA guidance, so a Sunday batch should be eaten or frozen by Thursday. Cool food quickly in shallow containers rather than leaving a full pot on the counter, and reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C).
