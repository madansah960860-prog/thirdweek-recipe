Kitchen basics & conversions

The measurements every Saltgrain recipe is written in, why a cup of flour can vary by 30 grams, and the charts we check against on the wall of the test kitchen.

The conventions we use

Every Saltgrain recipe is written in US customary measures first, because that is what our readers cook with. A metric toggle sits at the top of every recipe and converts every quantity on the page live. Where a quantity is precise enough to matter, the gram weight is printed beside the cup.

  • 1 US cup = 240 ml. This is the US legal cup used on nutrition labels. The US customary cup is technically 236.6 ml, and the difference is under two percent, which does not matter in cooking. We round to 240 ml and so does the FDA.
  • 1 US tablespoon = 15 ml. There are 16 tablespoons in a cup.
  • 1 US teaspoon = 5 ml. There are 3 teaspoons in a tablespoon.
  • 1 US fluid ounce = about 30 ml. Two tablespoons.
  • Oven temperatures are given in °F with the °C equivalent beside them, for example 425°F (220°C). Convection notes appear where they matter.
  • Weights are ounces and pounds, with grams beside them in baking recipes where accuracy changes the outcome.
  • Salt is kosher salt unless stated otherwise. If you are using fine table salt, use about half the volume, because the crystals are much smaller and pack more densely.

Worth knowing if you cook from more than one source: a UK or Australian cup is 250 ml and an Australian tablespoon is 20 ml, a third larger than the US one. Stacked across three or four tablespoons of a thickener or an acid, that gap is large enough to change a sauce.

Why a cup of flour is not a fixed weight

This is the most useful thing to understand about baking from American recipes. A cup measures volume. How much flour occupies that volume depends on how the flour got in there, and the spread is wide enough to break a recipe.

Dip the cup into the bag and level it off and you compact the flour, landing near 150 grams. Spoon it in loosely and level it and you land near 120 grams. Same cup, same flour, 25 percent more of it. In a cake batter that gap is the difference between tender and dry, and it is the first thing we check when a reader reports a failed bake.

Every recipe here that measures flour in cups is written for the spoon and level method and prints the gram weight alongside. If you own a scale, use the grams and stop thinking about it.

Convection adjustment. A convection oven moves hot air around the food, which cooks faster and more evenly. The standard adjustment is to reduce the temperature by 25°F, or about 15°C, and to start checking around five minutes early. Some newer ovens make this adjustment internally when you select convection; check your manual before doubling up on the reduction.

Your oven is probably not the temperature it says. Home ovens routinely run 15°F to 25°F off the dial, and they drift as they age. Both ovens in this kitchen read low, one of them by 20°F, which we only found by hanging a thermometer from the rack. That thermometer costs around $8 and is the cheapest improvement available to anyone who bakes.

How to check your own oven in ten minutes

  1. Hang an oven thermometer from the middle rack, at the center. Not on the floor of the oven, and not touching a wall.
  2. Set the dial to 350°F and wait twenty minutes after the preheat chime, not before it. Most ovens are still climbing when the chime sounds.
  3. Read the thermometer through the door glass. Opening the door drops the reading and wastes the wait.
  4. Repeat at 425°F. Ovens are rarely off by the same amount at both ends of the range, so note both figures.
  5. Write the two offsets on a piece of tape stuck to the oven door and adjust the dial by that much from then on.

If your reading is more than 50°F off, or the needle swings by more than 25°F while the oven holds a setting, the thermostat needs service rather than a mental correction.

Storage times and the two-hour rule

Perishable food should not sit at room temperature for more than two hours, or one hour when it is above 90°F (32°C). The temperature range between 40°F and 140°F (4°C and 60°C) is where bacteria multiply fastest.

General USDA refrigerator storage guidance at 40°F (4°C) or below: cooked poultry, meat and seafood, 3 to 4 days. Soups and stews, 3 to 4 days. Cooked rice and pasta, 3 to 5 days. Hard-boiled eggs, 1 week. Raw ground meat, 1 to 2 days. Raw poultry, 1 to 2 days. Raw steaks and chops, 3 to 5 days.

Cool hot food fast by dividing it into shallow containers rather than leaving one deep pot on the counter. A gallon of stew in a stockpot can stay in the danger zone for hours.

Substitute by volume, not by name. Two pans described the same way can differ by a third in capacity.

When you change pan size, the depth of the batter changes and so does the bake time. A deeper batter needs longer at a slightly lower temperature. Start checking at two thirds of the stated time.

Common ingredient substitutions

  • Buttermilk, 1 cup: 1 tablespoon lemon juice or white vinegar topped up with milk to 1 cup. Stand 10 minutes.
  • Self-rising flour, 1 cup: 1 cup all-purpose flour plus 1½ teaspoons baking powder and ¼ teaspoon salt.
  • Baking powder, 1 teaspoon: ¼ teaspoon baking soda plus ½ teaspoon cream of tartar. There is no reliable substitute the other way around.
  • Heavy cream for cooking, 1 cup: ¾ cup whole milk plus ¼ cup melted butter. This will not whip.
  • Cake flour, 1 cup: 1 cup all-purpose flour minus 2 tablespoons, plus 2 tablespoons cornstarch. Sift twice.
  • Superfine sugar, 1 cup: blitz 1 cup granulated sugar in a food processor for 30 seconds.
  • Fresh herbs to dried: use one third the amount. One tablespoon fresh oregano becomes one teaspoon dried.
  • Kosher salt to table salt: use about half the volume. Crystal size differs between brands, so taste as you go.

The three tools that fix most problems

An instant-read thermometer, a digital scale and an oven thermometer. Together they cost less than one decent skillet, and between them they remove almost every guess on this page.
