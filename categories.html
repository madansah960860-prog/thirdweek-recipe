How we decide what goes where

A recipe lives in exactly one category and may appear in one cuisine collection. The list is short on purpose. Forty overlapping tags is the same as no index at all.

  • Quick weeknight means 40 minutes or less, measured from walking into the kitchen. Marinating and chilling time counts. If a recipe needs you to have prepped on Sunday, it is not in here.
  • Baking and desserts covers anything where measurement precision changes the outcome, which is most sweet cooking and all bread.
  • One-pot and slow cooker means one vessel does the cooking. A recipe that needs a separate pot for pasta does not qualify, which is why the ragù sits here and the scampi does not.
  • Vegetarian and vegan covers recipes with no meat or fish as written. Where the recipe contains dairy or eggs, the vegan version is spelled out in the substitutions.

If you cannot find something, the full recipe index is filterable by course, cuisine, diet and total time.

Why the categories are shaped this way

Most recipe sites file by ingredient: chicken, pasta, beef, soup. That works if you already know what you want to cook. It is useless at six in the evening, when what you know is that you have forty minutes and one clean pan.

So we file by constraint instead. Time, equipment and who is eating are the three variables that decide a weeknight dinner, so they are the three shelves. If you do want to search by ingredient, the search box on the recipe index reads the ingredient list of every recipe on the site.

The cuisine collections work differently. They are less about narrowing a list and more about the few techniques and pantry items that carry a whole style of cooking. The Italian page spends most of its length on pasta water and canned tomatoes, because those two change more Italian dinners than any single recipe does. The Mexican and Tex-Mex page separates the two traditions and covers how to handle dried chilies. The Asian weeknights page admits the name is a compromise, and gives you the nine-item pantry that makes those dinners fast.

How the filing system works

Each recipe carries one category, at most one cuisine collection, and a set of machine-readable tags for course, diet and total time. The category decides which page the full method lives on. The tags decide whether the recipe survives a filter on the recipe index. Nothing is tagged twice to make a list look longer, so a filtered result of six recipes means there are six, not sixty with four that nearly qualify.

Where to start, depending on what you already know

  • You know the clock. Use the total time filter on the recipe index. It counts from a cold kitchen, with prep included.
  • You know the equipment. Start in one-pot and slow cooker, where every method is written for a single vessel.
  • You know who is eating. Start in vegetarian and vegan. The vegan version sits inside the method, not appended underneath it.
  • You know the ingredient. Use the search box on the recipe index. It reads titles and ingredient lists across all 24 recipes at once.

What is in each collection

Every collection page carries the full recipes, not just links. Where a recipe has its own detail page you get the servings scaler, the print layout and the Recipe structured data. Where it lives inside a collection you still get the ingredient checklist, the full method, the cook's tips and the storage notes, along with a page-level toggle that converts every quantity on the page between US customary and metric at once.

Nothing is held back for a paid tier and no collection is a stub. If a category is listed here, the recipes are on the page in full.
