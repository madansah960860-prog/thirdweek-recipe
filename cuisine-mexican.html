Mexican & Tex-Mex

Two related traditions that get filed as one. Here is what separates them, plus the four chilies, the tortilla timings and the two steps that changed our results most.

Tex-Mex is not bad Mexican food. It is a distinct regional American cuisine that developed among Tejano communities in Texas over roughly two centuries, and it runs on its own logic: yellow cheese, cumin, wheat flour tortillas, canned tomatoes and beef. Mexican cooking, across its many regional traditions, leans on dried chilies, corn, lime, fresh cheeses and a far wider range of preparations.

Both are worth cooking, and every recipe on this page is labeled so you know which one you are making. What they share is a test result: the two cheapest steps, warming the tortillas and toasting the chilies, are the ones most home cooks skip and the ones that moved our tasting scores furthest. Neither adds more than two minutes to a recipe.

Four dried chilies, ranked by what they do

Dried chilies are the base of most Mexican sauces. Most US supermarkets bag them in the produce or international aisle at $3 to $5 a bag. Judge them by feel: a good chili bends like leather, a stale one snaps. A chili that snaps has dried past its oils and will taste dusty no matter how long you soak it.

Four dried chilies: heat, flavor and best use
ChiliHeatTastes likeUse it for
Ancho (dried poblano)MildRaisin, dried plum, mild tobaccoThe base of most red sauces and moles
GuajilloMild to mediumGreen tea, tart berry, slight smokeAdobos, birria, red enchilada sauce
Chipotle (smoked jalapeño)Medium to hotHeavy smoke, vinegar when canned in adoboMarinades, bean dishes, cremas
ÁrbolHotClean, sharp, grassy heatSalsas where you want heat and not much else

Method: tear them open, discard the stems and most of the seeds, toast in a dry skillet 30 to 45 seconds a side until they smell nutty, then soak in hot water for 20 minutes and blend. Watch the clock rather than the color. Past about a minute a side they scorch, and scorched chili is bitter in a way no amount of sugar, salt or acid will pull back. We wrecked two batches of guajillo confirming that.

Tortillas: twenty seconds a side

Warm them. Twenty seconds a side directly over a gas flame, or in a dry skillet over medium-high until they puff and show a few brown spots. A cold tortilla out of the bag is stiff and tastes of packaging. A warmed one is pliable and tastes of corn. Stack them in a clean kitchen towel as they come off so the trapped steam keeps them soft. A stack held that way stays usable for about twenty minutes.

Corn tortillas are traditional for tacos in most of Mexico. Flour tortillas are the Tex-Mex and northern Mexican norm, and they are the right choice for burritos and quesadillas. Neither is more correct; they belong to different dishes.

Salsa is a category, not a recipe

Four types cover almost everything, and they differ in method and shelf life more than in ingredients.

  • Salsa cruda is raw: chopped tomato, onion, chili, cilantro, lime. Made in five minutes, eaten within an hour.
  • Salsa roja is cooked or blended from toasted dried chilies and tomato. Keeps a week.
  • Salsa verde uses tomatillos, either raw for a sharp bright sauce or roasted for something rounder.
  • Salsa macha is oil-based, with fried chilies, nuts and seeds. Keeps for a month refrigerated, and it is the one worth making on a Sunday.

How we tested this

The chili table came out of four tasting rounds in the Portland test kitchen. Each chili was toasted, soaked and blended on its own with water and salt only, so nothing else colored the result, then tasted blind by four people. Heat levels are relative to each other rather than absolute Scoville figures, because bag to bag variation in dried chilies is wide enough that a published number would be false precision. Tortilla timings were taken on both a gas flame and a dry carbon steel skillet, and they matched within five seconds.

Shopping notes

A Mexican grocery will have better and cheaper dried chilies, fresh masa if you are lucky, and queso fresco that has not sat in plastic for a month. Look for a tienda or a carniceria near you.

Otherwise, most Kroger and Safeway stores carry ancho, guajillo and árbol chilies, chipotles in adobo, tomatillos and queso fresco. Costco sells corn tortillas in quantities that only make sense if you freeze half.

Portland, OR

Locally we buy chilies by the bag at the tiendas along SE 82nd Avenue, where a bag runs closer to $2 and turns over fast enough to stay pliable. Several carniceria counters in the same stretch press corn tortillas through the morning, and a tortilla two hours old needs no reheating at all.

Tortillas freeze well for 3 months. Separate them with parchment before freezing or you will have one solid block.
