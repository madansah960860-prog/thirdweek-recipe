What we write about, and what we do not

Field notes exist for the material that will not fit inside a method. A recipe tells you to spread the vegetables in a single layer. An article can tell you why crowding causes steaming, what a single layer means in pounds per pan, and what to do when the only pan you own is too small.

We write about technique, ingredients and sourcing. We do not write about nutrition, diets, weight or health, because we are cooks and not qualified to, and because a recipe site that assigns moral weight to food does its readers a disservice. The words clean, guilt-free and sinful do not appear anywhere on this site.

Everything here is tested before it publishes, and an article is revised when a later test changes the advice. The date under each headline is the last revision, not the first publication.

Why these three came first

Each of these started as a repeated reader report. Traybakes arriving pale and wet from methods that had been followed exactly, so we put eleven trays through the oven and isolated the variable. Repeated questions about which can of tomatoes to buy, so we cooked the same sauce seven times and tasted it blind. Readers saying they felt slow and unsafe with a knife, so we timed three dice sizes and wrote down the four cuts that carry most home cooking.

That is the rule we intend to keep. A question that arrives three times becomes a test rather than three separate replies.

How an article gets written here

The same way a recipe does, which is to say slowly. We cook the thing repeatedly and we deliberately cook the version that fails, because a description of the failure is worth more than another description of success. Then somebody who did not write it reads it and marks every sentence they had to read twice. Those marks are usually where the missing number was.

Where we cite a temperature, a storage time or a safety rule, it comes from USDA or FDA guidance and we name the source in the text rather than asking you to take our word. Where something is preference rather than measurement, we say which it is. There is a real difference between browning butter changes the flavor and we prefer walnut pesto to pine nut pesto, and both belong on a cooking site as long as nobody is presenting the second as the first.

What is on the bench next

Currently in the testing queue, in no particular order: salting at each stage of cooking and why timing moves the result more than quantity does; what a rest actually does to a piece of meat, measured with a probe rather than asserted; buying and storing olive oil without wasting money; how to read a bag of flour, including what the protein percentage does and does not tell you; and cooking for one, built from scratch rather than scaled down badly from four.

If something keeps going wrong for you, tell us. Reader reports set most of this queue, and the more specific the report, the faster it becomes a test.

How to read a field note

Every piece here separates three things on purpose. A measurement is something we recorded off a probe, a scale or a timer, and it is written with the number attached. A cited rule comes from USDA or FDA guidance and is named as such. A preference is ours, and it is labeled as ours. If a claim in one of these articles carries no number and no source, treat it as the third kind and cook accordingly. Where a piece ends with a note on what we would test next, that section is an admission: it lists the things we believe but have not yet proved on the bench.
