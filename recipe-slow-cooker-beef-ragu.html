Slow-Cooker Beef Ragù with Pappardelle

Twenty-five minutes of knife and skillet work, eight hours of nothing, and enough sauce for three separate dinners. The browning step is the one that cannot be skipped.

Developed and retested in the Saltgrain test kitchen by Priya Raghunathan Published 03/09/2026 Last tested 08/22/2026
Prep time
25 minutes
Cook time
8 hours on low
Total time
8 hours 25 minutes
Yield
8 servings
Difficulty
Easy
Course
Dinner

One-pot & slow cooker Italian at home Batch cooking Allergens: wheat, milk

This is the recipe that does the heavy lifting for a whole week. A chuck roast, a soffritto, a can of tomatoes and a cup of red wine go into the slow cooker before eleven in the morning. By dinner the beef gives under a spoon and the sauce has gone dark and glossy with no attention from you after the first twenty-five minutes.

The step people want to skip is the browning, and it is the step that decides the result. Fifteen minutes in a hot skillet builds the roasted, savory base a slow cooker cannot produce on its own, because a slow cooker never gets hot enough to brown anything. If you have made a slow-cooker sauce that tasted flat and faintly sweet, that is the reason, and we proved it to ourselves in round one.

We make the full batch every time and split it three ways: pappardelle on Sunday, baked ziti on Tuesday, stuffed peppers on Thursday. All three are written out further down, with the oven temperatures. Freezing instructions are below as well, and the sauce is better on day two than on day one.

How we tested this

Three rounds in the same 6-quart oval slow cooker. Round one went in raw, with no browning at all, to measure what that step is actually worth. The sauce came out pale, thin and faintly sweet, and nobody asked for a second bowl. Round two browned the chuck in three batches and cooked the tomato paste for two full minutes before the wine, which is the version that tastes like a braise rather than a stew. Round three ran high for 5 hours against low for 8 on separate days. Both work. Low pulled the beef apart more cleanly and needed less reduction at the end, which is why the method says low.

Method

  1. Season and brown the beef

    Season the chuck chunks all over with the salt and pepper. Heat the olive oil in a large skillet over medium-high until it shimmers. Brown the beef in two or three batches, about three minutes per side, until each piece has a deep crust. Do not crowd the pan. Move the browned beef to the slow cooker.

  2. Cook the soffritto

    Lower the heat to medium and add the onion, carrot and celery to the same skillet with all the browned bits still in it. Cook for 8 to 10 minutes, stirring, until soft and beginning to color. Add the garlic and cook one more minute.

  3. Cook out the tomato paste

    Stir in the tomato paste and cook for two full minutes, scraping constantly. It will darken from red to brick and start to stick. That color change is flavor; raw tomato paste tastes tinny and stays that way for eight hours.

  4. Deglaze with wine

    Pour in the red wine and scrape every stuck piece off the bottom of the pan. Simmer for three to four minutes until it has reduced by about half, then tip the whole lot into the slow cooker over the beef.

  5. Add the tomatoes and set it going

    Crush the whole peeled tomatoes with your hands directly into the slow cooker, juice included. Add the broth, rosemary sprigs, bay leaf and oregano. Stir once. Cover and cook on low for 8 hours, or high for 5. Low is meaningfully better.

  6. Shred and reduce

    Fish out the rosemary stems and bay leaf. Shred the beef in the pot with two forks; it should give with almost no resistance. If the sauce looks thin, prop the lid open with a wooden spoon and cook on high for 30 minutes to let steam escape. Taste and adjust the salt now, not before.

  7. Cook the pasta and combine

    Boil the pappardelle in well-salted water one minute short of the package time. Reserve a cup of pasta water, drain, and toss the pasta with about half the ragù and a splash of that water in a wide pan over low heat for a minute. Serve with Parmesan, parsley and a thread of extra-virgin olive oil.

Food safety

Cook beef in a slow cooker until it is fork-tender and reaches at least 145°F (63°C); a long braise takes it far past that. Never start a slow cooker with frozen meat, because the pot spends too long in the 40°F to 140°F (4°C to 60°C) range where bacteria multiply. Cool leftovers in shallow containers and refrigerate within two hours, per USDA guidance.

Cook's tips

  • Cut the chuck yourself instead of buying pre-cut stew meat. Pre-cut packages are usually trimmed from several different muscles that cook at different rates, which is why some pieces stay tough. A whole roast is also cheaper per pound at Costco or a butcher counter.
  • Dice the soffritto small and cook it longer than feels necessary. Ten minutes, not four. The vegetables should be sweet and collapsing before anything else goes in, because after that the temperature never rises high enough to soften them further.
  • Do not add the pasta water before you taste. Slow cookers lose almost no liquid, so this sauce arrives wetter than a stovetop braise. Reduce first, then thin deliberately.
  • Sauce the pasta in a pan, never in a bowl. Thirty seconds of tossing over heat lets the pasta absorb sauce instead of sitting in a puddle of it. This is the single biggest difference between home pasta and restaurant pasta.

What goes wrong

  • A thin, watery sauce. Slow cookers lose almost no liquid, so this arrives wetter than a stovetop braise. Prop the lid open with a wooden spoon and run it on high for 30 minutes before you touch the salt.
  • Stringy beef after eight hours. The cut was too lean. Chuck carries the collagen that melts into the sauce; a round roast has none left to give.
  • Pasta sitting in a puddle. The sauce and the pasta met in a bowl. Toss the drained pappardelle with the ragù and a splash of pasta water in a wide pan over low heat for a minute instead.

Substitutions

  • No slow cooker: use a Dutch oven at 300°F (150°C), lid on, for 3 to 3½ hours. Check the liquid at the two-hour mark.
  • No wine: replace with an equal amount of beef broth plus 1 tablespoon red wine vinegar added at the end.
  • Pork instead of beef: a boneless pork shoulder of the same weight works beautifully and is usually cheaper. Same times.
  • Ground beef version: 2 pounds of ground beef, browned hard and drained, cuts the cook time to 4 hours on low. The texture is different but the flavor holds up.
  • Gluten-free: serve over polenta instead of pasta. Coarse polenta, 45 minutes, plenty of butter.

Make ahead, storage and reheating

  • Make ahead: the soffritto can be cooked up to two days ahead and refrigerated. The finished sauce is better on day two than on day one, so cook it the day before if the timing allows.
  • Storage: cool quickly by spreading the sauce in a shallow container, then refrigerate within two hours. USDA guidance for cooked beef and stews is three to four days at 40°F (4°C) or below.
  • Freezing: freezes very well for up to three months in portioned containers. Leave an inch of headspace. Thaw overnight in the refrigerator.
  • Reheating: reheat in a covered saucepan over medium-low with a splash of water, stirring, until it reaches 165°F (74°C) throughout. Reheat only the portion you are eating.

Three dinners from one pot

  • Sunday: pappardelle, as written above.
  • Tuesday: stir 2 cups of ragù through cooked ziti with ricotta and mozzarella, then bake at 375°F (190°C) for 25 minutes.
  • Thursday: spoon the last of it into halved bell peppers, top with breadcrumbs, and roast at 400°F (200°C) for 30 minutes.

A 6-quart slow cooker, a heavy skillet for browning, and a wide pan for finishing the pasta. The equipment guide covers what to look for.
