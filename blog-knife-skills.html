TECHNIQUE

Four cuts, one grip, and a board that stays put

This is not knife school and speed is not the objective. Even pieces cook evenly, uneven pieces give you a pan that is raw in one place and burnt in another. One sharp knife, a board that does not move, four shapes.

By Priya Raghunathan Published 07/29/2026 Reading, 8 minutes

Knife skills are usually taught as though speed were the objective. In a professional kitchen, where somebody dices forty pounds of onion before service, speed is exactly the objective. At home it is not. The reason to cut well at home is narrower and more useful: pieces of the same size finish at the same time, and pieces of different sizes produce one pan holding both raw food and burnt food.

That is the entire argument. Everything below is in service of it.

Before any cutting: two things that matter more than technique

A sharp knife

A dull knife is more dangerous than a sharp one. That sounds like a thing people repeat without checking, and it is straightforwardly true. A sharp blade bites where you set it down. A dull one skates across the skin of a tomato or an onion until you add force, and then it moves somewhere you did not choose, quickly.

Sharpening is a habit, not a purchase. A pull-through sharpener costs about $20 and is adequate for most home knives. A honing steel does not sharpen, it realigns the edge, and it wants ten seconds every few times you cook. The test we use: if the knife will not part a tomato skin under its own weight, it needs attention before you start.

A board that does not move

Wet a paper towel, wring it out, lay it flat under the board. The board stops sliding. Four seconds, no cost, and it removes the single most common way home cooks cut themselves.

Use a larger board than feels necessary. On a small board food falls off the edges, and chasing pieces across a counter is how people start rushing.

The grip

Thumb and index finger pinch the blade just in front of the handle, remaining fingers wrapped round the handle. This is the pinch grip, and it gives far more control than holding the handle alone for one reason: your hand is closer to where the cutting actually happens.

The other hand makes a claw: fingertips curled under, knuckles forward. The flat of the blade rests against those knuckles and they steer it. Because the fingertips sit behind the knuckles, the blade cannot physically reach them. It feels wrong for about a week and then it stops feeling like anything.

Cut one: the slice

The foundation of the other three. The knife travels forward and down, never straight down. A chef's knife has a curved belly and the cut happens as that curve rocks through the food. Straight downward pressure crushes instead of cutting, which is exactly why pressing a knife into a tomato flattens it.

For anything round, cut a flat side first and set the food on it. An onion half, a carrot with one face taken off, half a potato. A stable base means the food cannot roll, and food rolling under a blade is the second most common way people get hurt at a board.

Use for: onions for a soffritto, garlic, mushrooms, anything that will soften and disappear into a dish.

Cut two: the dice

A dice is three sets of slices at right angles to one another. Slice into planks. Stack the planks and slice into batons. Turn the batons ninety degrees and slice across into cubes. There is nothing else to it.

Size follows cook time, not preference. Quarter-inch dice for something that has to melt into a sauce in ten minutes. Half-inch for roasting in 25 minutes. One-inch for a braise running two hours. Every recipe here states the size because the number is load-bearing, and we have watched dishes fail on nothing else.

For an onion: halve it root to tip, peel, leave the root end intact, make horizontal cuts toward the root, then vertical cuts, then slice across. The root is holding the layers together while you work. It goes in the bin at the end.

Cut three: the chiffonade

For leafy herbs and greens. Stack the leaves, roll them into a tight cylinder, slice across into thin ribbons. Basil, mint, sage, kale and collards all take it.

One rule for soft herbs: cut once, at the last possible moment, with a sharp knife. Basil bruises where it is crushed and blackens at the cut edges within minutes. A dull blade crushes more; repeated chopping crushes most of all. One clean pass, straight into the dish.

For kale in a salad the ribbon width decides whether people finish the bowl. Quarter-inch strips eat easily. Torn hand-sized pieces are work. Our kale and walnut salad is built on that measurement.

Cut four: the rough chop

Roughly even, deliberately imprecise, fast. This covers more home cooking than the other three combined. A rough chop is correct whenever the pieces are going to be blended, puréed, long-braised or otherwise reduced to a state where shape has stopped mattering.

The error is spending eight minutes on a perfect brunoise for a soup that goes into a blender. Match precision to the job. If it is being blended, chop it roughly and move on.

What to do with your hands while you are not cutting

Two habits that take most of the chaos out of the counter.

Clear as you go. Keep a bowl beside the board for trimmings. Trips to the bin break rhythm, and a board covered in scraps is a smaller board.

Set everything out before you start. The professional term is mise en place, and it is not an affectation. It is the reason a four-minute stir-fry is possible at all: there is no gap in the middle in which to go and slice a pepper. For anything fast, cut everything first and line it up in the order it enters the pan.

A note on safety

Boards, raw meat and cross-contamination

Use a separate board for raw meat, poultry and seafood, or cut produce first and meat second. Wash boards, knives and hands with hot soapy water after any contact with raw meat, and never put cooked food back onto a board that held raw meat, in line with USDA guidance. Plastic boards can go in the dishwasher; wooden boards should be washed by hand and dried standing up. If your board has deep grooves that trap food, it is time to replace it.

If you cut yourself, stop cooking, wash the cut, cover it properly with a waterproof dressing and a glove, and throw away anything the blood touched. Nobody enjoys that part but it is not a judgment call.

None of this needs a class. Work through a bag of onions with the claw grip and a damp towel under the board and most of it is yours inside a week.

What we measured

The dice sizes above are not house preference, they came off a timer. We roasted the same carrot at 425°F cut three ways and logged when each reached a knife-tender center: quarter-inch at 14 minutes, half-inch at 25, one-inch at 40. Then we ran a mixed tray with all three sizes together, which is the way most people actually cut, and at 25 minutes the small pieces were scorched and the large ones were still firm at the center. That single tray is the argument for consistent cutting, and it is more convincing than any sentence about technique.

What we would test next: whether a serrated knife outperforms a honed chef's knife on soft ripe tomatoes for home cooks who sharpen rarely, which is most people. We would also like to time the damp-towel trick against a rubber-footed board across several counter surfaces, because a stone counter and a laminate one do not behave the same way and we have only tested one of them.
