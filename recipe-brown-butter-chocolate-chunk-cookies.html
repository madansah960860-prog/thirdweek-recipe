Brown Butter Chocolate Chunk Cookies

Browned butter, a dough rested at least 2 hours, and chopped bar chocolate so you get pools rather than tidy dots. Crisp edge, chewy middle, 55 g per cookie, no mixer.

Developed and retested in the Saltgrain test kitchen by Priya Raghunathan Published 05/21/2026 Last tested 09/03/2026
Prep time
20 minutes
Cook time
13 minutes
Total time
45 minutes plus 2 hours chilling
Yield
18 cookies
Difficulty
Easy
Course
Dessert

Baking & desserts Make ahead Allergens: wheat, milk, eggs, may contain soy and tree nuts from the chocolate

There are three decisions in this recipe and each one is doing measurable work. Browning the butter drives off water and leaves toasted milk solids that read as caramel and hazelnut. Resting the dough lets the flour hydrate and the sugars break down, so the cookie bakes darker and tastes less of raw flour. Chopping a bar instead of opening a bag of chips is the difference between pools of chocolate and small brown dots.

Browning the butter is the only step that needs your full attention, and it takes about six minutes. The butter melts, foams, quiets down, and then the solids on the bottom go from pale to golden to nut brown in roughly forty-five seconds. That last stretch is why you do not walk away. Use a light-colored pan so you can read the color, and pour it out the moment it smells toasty.

Browning removes water from the butter, so this dough looks drier than a standard cookie dough. That is expected, and it happened in every round we ran. Do not add liquid to correct it.

How we tested this

Five rounds, 18 cookies each, portioned at 55 g on a digital scale so every cookie on a pan finished at the same minute. Rounds one and two split a single bowl of dough between a 2-hour rest and a 24-hour rest. Both are good. The 24-hour batch baked darker at the edges and tasted less of flour, which is why the method gives you the range. Round three baked chips against chopped bar chocolate on the same pan, and that is the round after which we stopped calling for chips. Round four measured the flour by scooping the cup straight into the bag instead of spooning and levelling, came out roughly 20 percent heavy, and produced a cakey cookie, which is why there is a gram weight on the list. Round five baked two pans at once to find out whether that matters. It does. The lower pan browned unevenly, so the method says one pan at a time on the middle rack.

Ingredient checklist

01For the dough

Flour measured by scooping the cup straight into the bag can be 20 percent heavier than flour spooned in. If you own a scale, use 300 g.

02For the chocolate and finish

Chopped bar chocolate melts into pools and shards. Chips contain stabilisers that hold their shape, which is a different and less interesting cookie.

Method

  1. Brown the butter

    Melt the butter in a light-colored saucepan over medium heat. It will foam, then quiet down. Swirl the pan and keep watching: after four to six minutes the solids on the bottom turn golden brown and the smell turns nutty. Pour it immediately into a large bowl, scraping every brown speck in with it, and let it cool for 20 minutes until it is no longer hot to the touch.

  2. Beat in the sugars

    Whisk the brown sugar and superfine sugar into the cooled butter for a full minute. Add the eggs one at a time, whisking well after each, then the vanilla. Keep whisking for another 30 seconds until the mixture looks glossy and slightly lighter.

  3. Fold in the dry ingredients

    In a separate bowl, whisk the flour, baking soda and salt. Add to the wet mixture and fold with a spatula just until no dry streaks remain. Stop there. Overmixing develops gluten and gives you a tough, cakey cookie.

  4. Add the chocolate

    Fold in the chopped chocolate, including the dusty shards at the bottom of the cutting board. Those small pieces melt into the dough and darken the crumb.

  5. Rest the dough

    Cover the bowl and refrigerate for at least 2 hours, and up to 72 hours. Two hours makes a good cookie. Twenty-four hours makes a noticeably better one: deeper color, more caramel, less raw flour taste.

  6. Heat the oven and portion

    Heat the oven to 375°F (190°C), or 350°F (175°C) on convection. Line two sheet pans with parchment. Scoop 3-tablespoon balls of dough, about 55 g each, and space them at least 3 inches apart. Six to a pan. Sprinkle each with flaky salt.

  7. Bake

    Bake one pan at a time on the middle rack for 11 to 13 minutes, until the edges are set and deep golden and the centers still look slightly underdone and puffed. They will not look finished. Take them out anyway.

  8. Cool on the pan

    Let the cookies sit on the hot pan for 10 minutes; they finish cooking there and settle into their final texture. Move to a rack after that. If you want the bakery-style ripples, bang the pan firmly on the counter twice as soon as it comes out of the oven.

Food safety

This dough contains raw eggs and raw flour, neither of which is safe to eat uncooked. USDA and FDA guidance is clear that raw flour has been linked to illness as well as raw egg, so do not taste the dough or let children eat it. Bake fully, and refrigerate the dough between mixing and baking.

Baker's tips

  • Weigh the flour if you possibly can. 300 g is the target. Cup measurements for flour vary by up to 20 percent depending on how you fill the cup, and flour is the ingredient that decides whether a cookie spreads or stands up. Our conversions page lists cup weights for common ingredients.
  • Bake one sheet at a time on the middle rack. Two pans at once means the lower one browns unevenly and the upper one bakes faster. It feels wasteful and it is worth it.
  • Underbake by a minute. The residual heat of the pan carries the centers to done. A cookie that looks finished in the oven is a dry cookie on the plate.
  • Chill the second pan while the first bakes. Dough that warms up on the counter spreads into thin, greasy discs. Keep the waiting dough in the refrigerator.
  • Portion by weight for even bakes. 55 g per cookie means every cookie finishes at the same moment, which is the only way a 12-minute window works.

What goes wrong

  • Flat, greasy discs. The dough was warm going into the oven. Cool the browned butter the full 20 minutes before the sugars go in, and keep the second pan in the refrigerator while the first bakes.
  • Cakey, tough cookies. Either the flour was scooped rather than spooned and levelled, or the dough was mixed past the point where the dry streaks disappear. Weigh 300 g and stop folding early.
  • Dry cookies that looked right in the oven. They were baked to done instead of pulled underdone. Out at 11 to 13 minutes with the centers still puffed, then 10 minutes on the hot pan to finish.

Substitutions

  • No time to brown butter: use 1 cup melted, cooled unsalted butter. The cookie is still good, just less complex.
  • Dairy-free: a firm plant-based baking block works, but it will not brown. Melt it and add ½ teaspoon extra vanilla.
  • Milk chocolate: works, but drop the brown sugar to ¾ cup or the cookie tips into cloying.
  • Add nuts: 1 cup toasted, chopped walnuts or pecans folded in with the chocolate. Toast them first at 350°F (175°C) for 8 minutes.
  • Gluten-free: a cup-for-cup gluten-free blend with xanthan gum substitutes directly by weight. Rest the dough the full 24 hours; it matters more here.

Make ahead, storage and reheating

  • Make ahead: the dough keeps covered in the refrigerator for up to 72 hours and improves for the first 24.
  • Freezing dough: freeze portioned balls on a tray, then bag them for up to 3 months. Bake from frozen at the same temperature, adding 2 to 3 minutes.
  • Storage: baked cookies keep in an airtight container at room temperature for 4 days. A slice of sandwich bread in the container gives up its moisture to the cookies and keeps them soft.
  • Reheating: 4 minutes at 300°F (150°C) brings a day-old cookie most of the way back. Ten seconds in a microwave works if you are not proud.

What you need to bake them

A light-colored saucepan so you can judge the butter, a digital scale, two sheet pans and parchment. A stand mixer is not needed; a whisk and a spatula do the whole job. See the equipment guide for the scale we use.
