FROM THE TEST KITCHEN

Frequently asked questions

How the testing works, how the measurements are decided, and how the site pays for itself. Answered at length, because short answers are where the useful part gets lost. If yours is not here, write to us.

Yes. Every recipe, article and guide here is free to read. No article limit, no registration wall, no email required to see a method. The site is paid for by display advertising, affiliate commissions, optional paid downloads and an optional membership. The line-by-line breakdown is on the about page.

It means three complete cooks, logged, before anything is published. Round one is the draft: the developer cooks it as written and records pan size, rack position, oven temperature, stage timings and the point at which it went off. Round two changes one variable and cooks it again. Round three is cooked by somebody who did not write the recipe and has only the written method to work from, with no verbal shortcuts and nobody standing over the pan.

If any round fails, the count resets. Three passes have to be consecutive. The third round is the one that matters most, because it is the only one that catches an instruction which made sense inside the writer’s head. In practice the correction is usually a missing pan diameter or a visual cue nobody thought to write down.

Because a cup of flour is not a fixed quantity. Scooped straight from the bag it can weigh 145 g; spooned in and levelled it can weigh 120 g. That is a swing of around 20 percent, and in a cookie dough or a pancake batter it is the difference between tender and tough. A scale removes the variable entirely.

We keep the cup measurements because most US kitchens have cups and not everyone wants to buy a scale. Where volume measurement is genuinely risky, the recipe says so in the ingredient line. For baking we would rather you used the gram figure, and the gram figure is the one we tested to.

Because they pay for the groceries. Three rounds of testing means buying the ingredients three times, and photography runs about a day per recipe. Advertising is the largest single line in our income and it is what keeps the methods free to read.

We keep it contained. Ads are labeled, they sit between sections rather than above the recipe, they never expand over the method, and there are none at all on the legal pages or the error page. If you would rather not see them, the $5 membership removes them entirely.

Sometimes, yes. Some product links are affiliate links, which means we may receive a commission if you buy through them. It costs you nothing extra.

The disclosure sits at the point of every link, not only in the footer. Nothing reaches our equipment page unless it is in this kitchen and has been used through at least one full test cycle, and we do not accept payment in exchange for a recommendation. Full details are in the advertising and affiliate disclosure.

Because it says so at the very top of the page, above the headline, before you read anything else. We do not put sponsorship labels at the bottom.

A sponsor pays us to develop a recipe around their product. The recipe still clears three rounds of testing. Sponsors do not review our copy, cannot change a temperature, a timing or a verdict, and if we cannot produce something that passes on its own merits we do not publish and we return the fee.

Yes. Every recipe page has a measurement toggle at the top. Switch it to metric and every quantity on the page converts immediately, and the setting follows you from recipe to recipe.

We use 1 US cup = 240 ml and 1 US tablespoon = 15 ml. A UK or Australian cup is 250 ml and an Australian tablespoon is 20 ml, which matters if you are cooking from several sources at once. Full conversion tables are on the kitchen basics page.

Use the servings scaler on any recipe page and every quantity recalculates.

Two things do not scale. Cooking time does not: doubling a traybake does not double the roasting time, it means you need a second pan, because the limit is surface area and not mass. And baking scales less predictably than savory cooking, because leavening, pan depth and surface area interact. For baking, two separate batches beat one doubled one.

First, measure it. Hang a separate oven thermometer in the middle of the center rack, set the oven to 350°F, give it 20 minutes past the preheat beep and read it. Most domestic ovens are out by 15°F to 25°F, and a fair number are out by more. We check ours against a hanging thermometer every quarter and they hold to within about 10°F.

Once you know the offset, correct the dial rather than the recipe. If yours reads 375°F when set to 350°F, set it to 325°F and it will run at the temperature the method was tested at. Where a dish is sensitive to a small error, the recipe gives you a visual or probe target as well as a time, and the probe target is the one to trust.

Because it is an estimate and we do not want it read as a measurement. The figures come from generic ingredient databases, not laboratory analysis of the food we cooked. Brands vary, cuts of meat vary, and the amount of oil that stays in the pan rather than on the plate varies.

We are a cooking site, not a nutrition service. We do not put calorie counts in navigation or headlines, we do not frame recipes around restriction, and we do not describe food as clean or guilt-free. Read the nutrition and allergen disclaimer, and talk to a qualified health professional about anything that matters medically.

The tags describe the recipe as written, using the nine major food allergens recognized in the United States: milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame.

They cannot account for the specific products in your kitchen. Curry pastes frequently contain shrimp, soy sauce usually contains wheat, and cross-contamination during manufacturing is common and disclosed only on the packet. If you are cooking for someone with an allergy, read every label yourself, every time. Our tag is a starting point, not a clearance.

Most recipes carry a substitutions section listing the swaps we have cooked ourselves, including dairy-free, gluten-free and vegan versions where they hold up.

Where a substitution does not work we name it rather than staying quiet. Light coconut milk in a curry broth, for example, gives a thin and faintly chalky result, and no amount of reducing or adjusting recovers it. A swap we have not tested is not listed, even when it seems obvious.

The same way we validate the recipe. A listed substitution has been cooked as its own version, back to back with the original, on the same day, in the same pan, with one ingredient changed and nothing else. Two cooks taste both and record what moved: texture, seasoning level, browning, set, and how long it took to get there.

If the substitute version needs a different quantity, a different temperature or a different time, the substitution note carries those numbers. If it needs an adjustment we cannot describe precisely, it does not get published as a swap. That is why our substitution lists are shorter than most, and why the entries on them are specific.

Every recipe carries a storage and reheating section with times for that dish. In general we follow USDA guidance: cooked poultry, meat and seafood keep three to four days at 40°F (4°C) or below, and leftovers should be reheated to 165°F (74°C).

The step people skip is cooling. Get food into shallow containers and into the refrigerator within two hours, or one hour if the room is above 90°F (32°C). A deep pot left on the counter spends hours in the range where bacteria multiply, and nothing you do later undoes that.

Yes. There is a print button at the top of every recipe. The print version drops the navigation, the ads, the full-size photographs and everything else you do not need at the stove, leaving the ingredients, the method and the notes. Members get a designed recipe card as well.

Yes, on the same device and browser. Ticks are stored locally on your own machine and never reach our servers, so you can refresh a page mid-cook without losing your place. Every recipe has a clear ticks button. Clearing your browser storage clears them too.

It is the date the current method was last cooked in this kitchen, not the date the page was published or last edited. If we fix a typo the date does not move. If we change a temperature, a timing, a pan size or a quantity, the recipe goes back through testing and the date moves with it.

Read an old date carefully. A recipe that has sat for two years without revision is not proven stable, it is simply untouched. Where an ingredient has changed in the market since we tested, which happens with flours, canned tomatoes and commercial stocks, we would rather retest than leave the date standing.

Yes, and every request is read. We cannot promise to take one on, because a recipe takes about a week of bench time to get through three rounds, but requests shape the testing queue directly. Several recipes here exist because enough people asked for the same thing.

Tell us. Use the contact form and choose the correction subject. Give us the recipe name, the oven temperature you set, the pan and its diameter, the rack position, the elapsed time at the point it went wrong, and one sentence describing what you saw.

If two or more people report the same fault it goes back on the bench. When we change a method we change the last-tested date at the top, so a correction is visible rather than quietly absorbed.

Still stuck?

The kitchen basics page carries the conversion tables, oven charts, safe cooking temperatures and storage times. The equipment and pantry page covers what is worth buying and what is not. If none of that settles it, the contact form reaches one of three people, not a queue.

For a recipe problem, send us the numbers: oven temperature set, pan material and diameter, rack position counted from the bottom, elapsed time at the point it went wrong, and any internal temperature you measured. That is nearly always enough for us to reproduce the fault on the bench and tell you what happened.
