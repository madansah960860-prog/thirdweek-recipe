SOURCING

The tomato aisle, product by product

Six products share one shelf under names that imply they are interchangeable. They are not. Here is what separates them, what each one is engineered to do, and which can we reach for in which sauce.

By Wes Brandt Published 08/21/2026 Reading, 7 minutes

For most of the year a canned tomato beats a fresh one. Canning tomatoes are picked ripe and processed within hours, which is not true of the pale things sold in February for slicing. A can of good whole peeled tomatoes costs about $2.50, and it appears in more dinners out of this kitchen than any other single item on the shelf.

The problem is that the aisle holds at least six distinct products under near-identical names, and substituting one for another changes the result in ways most recipes never mention. Below is the whole aisle, ordered by how much processing each product has had.

Whole peeled tomatoes

Tomatoes peeled and packed whole, usually in their own juice or a light purée. Least processed, most useful, because the texture decision stays with you. Crush them by hand in the pot and you get a coarse sauce with visible pieces. Blitz them and you get smooth. Simmer them whole and break them down with a spoon as they soften and you land between the two.

Whole peeled also tells you the most about what you bought. Tip a can into a bowl: you want four or five intact tomatoes, not a slurry carrying a few fragments. Firm, whole, deep red fruit means the packer used good tomatoes. A can that is mostly liquid around pale mush was packed from seconds, and no amount of cooking recovers it.

Use for: long-simmered sauces, braises, seafood stews, anything where you want the sauce to have texture. This is the can to default to.

Crushed tomatoes

Whole tomatoes run through a crusher and packed with added tomato purée as a thickener. Convenient, consistent, and slightly sweeter than whole peeled, because the purée doing the thickening has already been cooked down.

The cost is control. You cannot un-crush them, and the texture swings hard between brands: some crushed tomatoes arrive nearly chunky, others nearly smooth. Where a method depends on a specific texture, whole peeled is the safer can.

Use for: pizza sauce, quick weeknight sauces, chili, anything where you want to open a can and go.

Diced tomatoes

The one worth understanding properly. Diced tomatoes are treated with calcium chloride, a firming agent that stops the cubes collapsing. That is not a side effect, it is the entire design brief: the pieces hold their shape.

Which is exactly right when you want visible tomato in a finished dish and exactly wrong when you want a sauce. Those cubes are still cubes after 90 minutes of simmering. A sauce that never comes together is, more often than any other cause we see reported, a diced tomato doing the job of a whole peeled one.

Use for: chunky salsas, soups where you want distinct pieces, chili with visible tomato. Do not use for: smooth sauces.

Tomato purée

This is where the transatlantic naming collision starts. In the United States, tomato purée is a cooked, strained, thick product sitting between crushed tomatoes and paste. In the United Kingdom, tomato purée means concentrated tomato paste in a tube, a far more concentrated thing entirely.

So a British method calling for two tablespoons of tomato purée means American tomato paste. Use two tablespoons of American purée instead and the dish arrives thin and underseasoned, and nothing in the written method will tell you why.

Use for: smooth sauces where you want body without long cooking. It is already cooked, so it needs less simmering.

Passata

Uncooked tomatoes, strained to remove skins and seeds, bottled raw. An Italian product, now widely stocked in US supermarkets, usually in glass rather than a can. Uncooked means it tastes brighter than purée; strained means it is completely smooth. Both of those are the point.

Use for: quick sauces, pizza sauce that goes on raw, anything where you want smooth texture and bright flavor without an hour of cooking.

Tomato paste

Tomatoes cooked down for hours until nearly all the water is gone, then strained. Intensely concentrated, deep red-brown, and useful well beyond tomato flavor: it carries glutamates, which is to say savouriness, into almost anything.

The step that matters is cooking it out. Two minutes of paste pressed against the bottom of a hot pan, stirred, until it darkens from bright red to brick. Raw paste stirred straight into liquid reads tinny and metallic and stays that way for the whole cook. This is one of the few single steps we have watched change a dish outright in side-by-side testing.

Buy it in a tube rather than a can where you can. A can commits you to six tablespoons. A tube lets you use one and refrigerate the rest, where it keeps for months.

Use for: building depth at the start of braises, stews, ragùs and chili. Almost never as the main tomato element.

What about San Marzano

San Marzano is a variety of plum tomato. The genuine article carries a DOP seal confirming it was grown in a defined area of Campania. Those cans run roughly twice the price of a standard can.

Our position, stated plainly: they are very good, and the gap is narrower than the price gap suggests once the pot also contains garlic, chili, herbs and forty minutes of cooking. Where they earn the premium is a three-ingredient sauce, where the tomato is carrying the dish on its own.

Worth knowing as well: plenty of cans read San Marzano without a DOP seal, meaning the variety was grown elsewhere. That is not fraud and those tomatoes are often excellent, but you are not buying what the price implies you are buying. Check for the seal, or buy a good domestic brand and put the difference into olive oil.

A short buying checklist

  • Default to whole peeled unless the recipe specifically wants pieces to hold their shape.
  • Check the ingredient list. Tomatoes, tomato juice and salt is what you want. Added sugar and calcium chloride tell you what the product is for.
  • Buy in quantity when you find a brand you like. Cans keep for two years and the price per can at Costco is often half the supermarket price.
  • Once opened, move leftover tomatoes out of the can into a covered container and refrigerate. They keep five to seven days, or freeze in an ice cube tray.
  • A dented can is fine. A can that is bulging, leaking or rusted through should be thrown away without opening, per USDA guidance.

To see the choices applied, the slow-cooker ragù uses whole peeled crushed by hand and cooks the paste out properly, and the seafood stew takes crushed for speed. Both methods state the reason at the point the can goes in.

What we measured

This piece came out of a blind tasting run over two days. We cooked one 40-minute sauce recipe seven times, changing only the can: two brands of whole peeled, one crushed, one diced, one US purée, one passata and one DOP San Marzano. Same pan, same quantity, same heat, same salt. Three of us tasted without seeing the labels. Diced was identified by every taster on texture alone and nobody scored it as a sauce. The DOP can placed first, but only two of three tasters picked it out, and the gap closed to nothing when the same cans were retested in a sauce carrying garlic and chili.

What we would test next: whether the calcium chloride in diced tomatoes has any measurable effect on a chili cooked past three hours, which is the one long-cook application where several readers insist diced works. We would also like to run passata against US purée in a raw pizza sauce, held overnight, to see whether the brightness advantage survives a night in the refrigerator. Neither has been cooked three times yet, so neither is in the guide above.
