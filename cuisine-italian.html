Tested 3 times

Italian at home

Eleven recipes and the five variables we measured until the results stopped moving: salt per quart, reserved starch water, drain timing, tomato type, garlic count.

Italian home cooking has few moving parts, which makes it easy to test. Cook the same dish four ways and one variable explains most of the gap between the batches: pasta finished in the pan, in the sauce, with a ladle of its own starchy cooking water. Across these eleven recipes, that step changed the plate more than any ingredient upgrade we paid for.

The second result is that ingredient count works against you. Four ingredients that are all good beat eleven that are mostly fine. Cacio e pepe has three. Panzanella has five that count. Where a recipe on this page has a short list, that is a finding and not a style.

The third is that acid and salt are corrections, not decorations. Taste at the end, off the heat, and expect to add something. Every dish here gets seasoned twice: once in the water, once in the bowl.

None of it needs ingredients you cannot get at a Kroger or a Trader Joe's. Where a specific product measurably changed the outcome in testing, we name it and say what changed, on the pantry page.

Five variables, measured

Salt: 1 tablespoon per 4 quarts

One tablespoon of kosher salt per four quarts of water. Pasta takes on salt once, and this is the window. Water that tastes flat gives you pasta that tastes flat regardless of the sauce on top. The exception is cacio e pepe and other cheese-led sauces: the pecorino carries its own salt, so drop to two teaspoons or the finished bowl reads sharp.

Reserved water: half a cup, before draining

Scoop half a cup out of the pot before the colander, every time. The dissolved starch is an emulsifier: it holds fat and water together into a sauce that coats. Skip it and the oil or butter separates and pools at the bottom of the bowl inside a minute. This is the mechanism, not a refinement.

Timing: drain one minute early

Pull the pasta one minute short of the box time, move it into the sauce, and toss over low heat for sixty seconds with a splash of that water. It finishes cooking in the sauce and absorbs it. Plated against sauce spooned over fully drained pasta, testers picked the pan-finished bowl in eight of twelve blind tastings.

Tomatoes: read the label, not the brand

Whole peeled tomatoes crushed by hand give you texture and control. Crushed tomatoes are faster and slightly sweeter. Passata is smooth and suits thin sauces. Tomato purée in the United States is a thick cooked product, which is not what the word means in Britain, so a British recipe calling for purée wants US tomato paste. Our guide to canned tomatoes has the full comparison.

Garlic: two cloves, sliced, pale gold

Two cloves, sliced, cooked in oil over medium-low until pale gold, roughly ninety seconds. Not six cloves, minced, dropped into a smoking pan. Minced garlic scorches in about twenty seconds over high heat, and scorched garlic is bitter enough to dominate everything else on the plate. It is the most common failure in the photos readers send us.

How we tested this

Every recipe on this page was cooked at least three times in the Portland test kitchen before it went up: a 30-inch gas range, a 12-inch stainless skillet, a 6-quart pot, an oven checked against a probe thermometer. When a step changed between rounds we reran the whole recipe rather than patching the line. Salt is measured as Diamond Crystal kosher. If you use Morton, use about two thirds by volume for the same result.

Pantry: the short list

  • Good olive oil for finishing, cheaper olive oil for cooking
  • Canned whole peeled tomatoes
  • Dried pasta in three shapes: long, short, and ribbon
  • Parmigiano Reggiano and Pecorino Romano, in the piece
  • Anchovies in oil
  • Dried oregano and red pepper flakes
  • Red wine vinegar
  • Canned white beans
  • Tuna packed in olive oil

Every item here keeps for months at room temperature or in the fridge door, and nine of the eleven recipes above are built out of it. Brands, prices and the tasting notes behind them are on the pantry page.

What this collection is not

These recipes were developed and tested in Portland, Oregon. They are not recipes from any specific Italian region and we will not claim otherwise. Where a dish has a traditional form we state it, then state our departure. Our panzanella uses mozzarella, which a Tuscan cook would find strange. Our pesto uses walnuts, which a Ligurian cook would find worse. Walnuts won our blind tasting on cost, and they held their flavor longer in the freezer.

That is the honest way to write about somebody else's food: cook it repeatedly, record what you changed, and do not claim authority you have not earned.

Where these recipes fail

  • Sauce slides off the pasta. The pasta was drained dry and dressed in a bowl. Keep half a cup of the water and finish in the pan.
  • A bitter note under everything. Garlic went in too hot or cut too fine. Slice it, start it in cool oil, pull the pan at pale gold.
  • Thin, sharp tomato sauce. Under-salted and under-cooked. Canned tomatoes need fifteen to twenty minutes at a bare simmer before the acid settles.
  • Grainy cheese sauce. The pan was too hot when the cheese went in. Off the heat, water first, cheese second, toss hard.
  • Collapsed panzanella. The bread was fresh. It needs to be a day old and dried out, or it turns to paste in the dressing inside ten minutes.
