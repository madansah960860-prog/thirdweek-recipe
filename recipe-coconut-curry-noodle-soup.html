Coconut Curry Chicken Noodle Soup

One pot, one can of full-fat coconut milk, thirty-five minutes, and a broth that tastes longer-cooked than it is because the curry paste is fried before any liquid goes near it.

Developed and retested in the Saltgrain test kitchen by Priya Raghunathan Published 02/18/2026 Last tested 09/02/2026
Prep time
15 minutes
Cook time
20 minutes
Total time
35 minutes
Yield
4 servings
Difficulty
Easy
Course
Dinner

One pot Asian weeknights Under 40 minutes Allergens: fish, may contain shellfish, soy, peanuts and tree nuts

This is a weeknight soup that borrows its structure from Thai curry noodle soups and does not pretend to be a traditional recipe from any one place. It uses jarred red curry paste, which is what most cooks in most kitchens actually reach for, and it leans on three finishing ingredients, lime, cilantro and scallion, to bring the pot back up at the end.

The step that carries the whole pot is frying the curry paste. Two minutes in hot oil with the aromatics, before any liquid, blooms the dried chilies and spices and moves the flavor from sharp to round. Tip the paste straight into broth and you get a soup that tastes like it came out of a jar, because functionally it did.

Cook the noodles in a separate pot. Rice noodles left standing in hot broth keep drinking it, so a pot that was right at dinner is a solid block by the time the leftovers go into the refrigerator. Cook them, rinse them cold, and put them in the bowl.

How we tested this

Three rounds in a 5-quart Dutch oven, plus one side test on curry paste. Round one stirred the paste straight into the broth without frying it: the result was flat and tasted faintly raw, which is how a two-minute fry ended up written into step two. Round two held the pot at a hard boil while the chicken poached, and the coconut milk separated into fat and grain while the thighs went stringy. Round three dropped to a bare simmer and fixed both at once. We then built the same broth with three brands of red curry paste at the same 3 tablespoons. One of the three was hot enough to be unpleasant, which is why the ingredient list tells you to start with two and decide later.

Method

  1. Soften the aromatics

    Heat the oil in a large Dutch oven or soup pot over medium. Add the onion and cook 5 minutes until translucent. Add the garlic and ginger and cook one more minute, stirring so the garlic does not catch.

  2. Fry the curry paste

    Add the curry paste and cook for 2 full minutes, pressing it against the bottom of the pot and stirring. It will darken slightly and smell noticeably deeper. This is the step that separates this soup from a bland one.

  3. Build the broth

    Pour in the coconut milk and stir until the paste dissolves completely with no orange streaks left. Add the broth, fish sauce and brown sugar. Bring to a gentle simmer, not a boil; hard boiling can split coconut milk.

  4. Poach the chicken

    Slide in the chicken strips and simmer for 8 to 10 minutes, until cooked through to 165°F (74°C). Keep the heat low enough that the surface barely moves. Boiling chicken thighs makes them stringy.

  5. Cook the noodles separately

    While the chicken poaches, cook the rice noodles in a separate pot according to the package, usually 4 to 6 minutes. Drain and rinse under cold water to stop them cooking, then divide between four bowls.

  6. Add the vegetables

    Add the bell pepper and snow peas to the broth and cook 2 minutes, until bright and still crisp. Turn off the heat and stir in the spinach; it wilts in the residual heat in about thirty seconds.

  7. Finish and serve

    Off the heat, stir in the lime juice. Taste and adjust with more fish sauce for salt or more lime for brightness. Ladle over the noodles and top with cilantro, scallions, peanuts and chili.

Food safety

Cook chicken to an internal temperature of 165°F (74°C) measured at the thickest point, in line with USDA guidance. Fish sauce contains anchovy, a fish allergen, and many red curry pastes contain shrimp paste, a shellfish allergen; read the label if you are cooking for someone with an allergy. Cool the soup quickly in a shallow container and refrigerate within two hours.

Cook's tips

  • Taste your curry paste before you commit. Heat levels vary enormously between brands, by a factor of three or more. Start with 2 tablespoons, taste the broth once it is built, and stir in the third if it needs it. You cannot take heat back out.
  • Buy coconut milk, not coconut beverage. The can in the international aisle, not the carton by the milk. Shake it hard before opening; the fat separates and sits at the top.
  • Rinse the noodles. Rice noodles keep cooking in their own residual starch and heat. A cold rinse stops that dead and keeps them separate rather than welded together.
  • Season the broth at the very end, not at the start. Fish sauce concentrates as the pot simmers. Adjusting after the vegetables go in gives you an accurate read.

What goes wrong

  • A broth that looks split or grainy. It boiled. Once the coconut milk is in, keep the surface barely moving for the rest of the cook.
  • Noodles welded into one block. They sat in the broth. Cook them in their own pot, rinse them under cold water, and ladle the broth over them in the bowl.
  • Thin, flat flavor. Either the paste never got fried or the can was light coconut milk. Two full minutes in the oil, and full fat only.

Substitutions

  • Vegetarian and vegan: use vegetable broth, swap the fish sauce for 1 tablespoon soy sauce plus ½ teaspoon rice vinegar, and replace the chicken with 14 ounces of firm tofu, cubed and added in the last 5 minutes. Check that your curry paste is shrimp-free; many brands are not.
  • Shrimp instead of chicken: add 1 pound of peeled shrimp in the final 3 minutes, cooked to 145°F (63°C).
  • Yellow or green curry paste both work. Green is usually hotter, yellow is milder and slightly sweeter.
  • No rice noodles: ramen noodles, egg noodles or even spaghetti will carry the broth. Cook and rinse them the same way.
  • Peanut-free: leave out the peanuts and top with toasted sesame seeds or crisp fried shallots instead.

Make ahead, storage and reheating

  • Make ahead: the broth base, without the vegetables or noodles, can be made 3 days ahead. It is better on day two than on day one.
  • Storage: store broth and noodles in separate airtight containers, refrigerated within two hours. USDA guidance for cooked poultry and soups is three to four days at 40°F (4°C) or below.
  • Freezing: the broth freezes for 2 months. Coconut milk can look grainy after thawing; whisking it over low heat brings it back. Do not freeze the noodles.
  • Reheating: reheat the broth to a bare simmer, at least 165°F (74°C), then pour it over fresh or rinsed noodles. Do not reheat with the noodles in the pot.

What you need to cook it

A 5-quart Dutch oven or heavy soup pot, a second pot for the noodles, and a microplane for the ginger. See the equipment and pantry page for the curry pastes and fish sauces we keep in the cupboard.
